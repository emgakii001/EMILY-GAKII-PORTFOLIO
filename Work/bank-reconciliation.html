Bank Reconciliation Case Study

The "missing" KES 7.9 million wasn't missing — it was recorded twice, two different ways.

A real bank reconciliation for a Kenyan agricultural cooperative, redone from source data. What looked like an unexplained cash discrepancy turned out to be a matching-method problem — and finding that difference is exactly the kind of thing that keeps a small business's numbers trustworthy.

Folio 01 — The Business Problem

A reconciliation had already been done. It just hadn't been checked.

The organisation is a savings-and-credit cooperative that moves a lot of money in and out every month — paying dairy and tea farmers for their produce, running float transfers between accounts, handling payroll deductions and county government remittances. Someone had already built a bank reconciliation for March 2026, matching the cash book against the bank statement line by line. It flagged 400 unresolved differences out of 1,553 line pairs, including one gap of nearly KES 7.8 million with no explanation attached.

Why this matters to a small business

A reconciliation that only checks whether two totals happen to match isn't actually protecting you. If a real KES 7.8M gap goes unexplained, you can't tell whether it's a timing difference, a bank error, a bookkeeping mistake, or something worse. The business needs to know which one it is — not just that a number doesn't tie out.

What I was asked to determine

Whether the existing reconciliation's 400 flagged differences were genuine reconciling items — or artefacts of how the comparison had been built. And, where a real gap existed, to trace it back to its source rather than leaving it as a line item marked "unexplained."

Folio 03 — The Approach

Verify the method before you verify the numbers

Before deciding whether the 400 flagged items were real problems, I needed to understand how the existing reconciliation had actually paired transactions — because a reconciliation is only as trustworthy as its matching logic.

01

Reverse-engineered the pairing logic

The existing reconciliation didn't match transactions by reference number or description — it sorted each side by amount and lined them up by row position. That's a reasonable shortcut when every transaction is recorded the same way on both sides. It breaks the moment it isn't.

↓
02

Classified every non-zero row before trusting any of it

Split the 400 flagged rows into what they actually were: rounding noise under one shilling, items missing entirely from one side, and rows where both sides had a transaction but the amounts genuinely disagreed. Only that last group could possibly be a real reconciling item.

↓
03

Traced the largest gaps back to source

For each material variance, I searched the full unmatched list for a companion entry — a second bank transaction or a second cash book line that, added to the first, would explain the gap. Three of the largest "differences" resolved this way, to the cent.

↓
04

Left what I couldn't prove as open, not closed

A handful of pairings had no real connection between the two sides at all — a payroll deduction lined up against an unrelated cooperative payment purely because the amounts were similar. I flagged those as false matches rather than forcing an explanation the data didn't support.

Folio 04 — Key Findings

The reconciliation's biggest "gap" was a matching problem, not a cash problem

Here is the largest and clearest example, shown as it appeared in the working papers.

Position-based matching assumes every transaction has exactly one counterpart on the other side. Whenever that assumption breaks — a split payment, a combined deposit — the matching drifts for every row that follows it, creating false variances on transactions that were never actually wrong.

What was left genuinely open

Not everything resolved cleanly, and I said so rather than forcing an answer.

  • ✓
    Two internal records posted directly against the bank statement

    A small county-government remittance and a payroll payment each showed a consistent small variance (KES 36–450) against a matching bank description — a genuine discrepancy worth a follow-up call to the bank, not a matching artefact.

  • !
    Several payroll deduction entries had no real bank counterpart

    The existing reconciliation paired them against unrelated cooperative payments purely because the amounts were close. I flagged these as likely false matches rather than accepting the pairing — the true bank entry for these items, if it exists in this statement, hasn't been identified yet.

  • i
    No opening or closing balance in the source extract

    This means individual transactions can be verified against each other, but the reconciliation can't yet be tied to a confirmed month-end bank balance. That figure would be the next document requested from the bank to close this file out completely.

Folio 05 — Outcome

From "400 unexplained differences" to a working list of 10 genuine items to resolve, plus KES 17.5M in real transactions ready to be posted.

The reconciliation didn't just get double-checked — it got rebuilt around what the data could actually prove. Every figure carried into the final list is one I can trace back to a source transaction, not one I inferred because the totals happened to line up.

Folio 06 — What I'd tell a small-business owner

A reconciliation that always balances isn't proof your books are right

Check the method, not just the total

Two totals matching doesn't mean every transaction behind them matched correctly. A quick check of how the pairing was actually done catches problems a summary figure hides.

Split payments need a rule

If a customer, farmer, or vendor payment sometimes arrives in two parts, decide up front how it gets recorded — combined or split, but the same way every time — so reconciliation doesn't have to guess.

Small charges add up unseen

KES 1.35M in bank charges sat unbooked in one month alone. None of it was dramatic on its own — it only became visible once someone reconciled the detail, not just the balance.
